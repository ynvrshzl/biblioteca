Code is perhaps, years ahead of our capacity to understand it. This follows a trend in history, from Mathematics to Sciences. However, we are approaching a transient edge in which we can no longer process the vast amount of digital information noise.

I believe, "As we may think" is perhaps one of the most important literary pieces of the past century, primarily becuase it unifies Science, War, Society all interconnected systems, unravelling eventually into the Phenomenological; "How does a person deal with information?"

That may be the defining class of Vanevar Bush's Essay. Most especially with today's unsustainable explosion of information. It is clear, that throughout human history, we made progressively paced movement toward a valued direction; in the ethical sense, a "Telos." This may be conceptualized as an abstract variable "T."

The complexity of information "C:I" is not merely combinatorial; it is experiential. Information is processed by the nervous system.

The context behind these ideas is a personal local experience with unmanageable complexity. Recently, I was studying plugin development, (...to build a personal extension for Obsidian one of my favorite information management applications, more on this later.) I believed the plugin would be as simple as "files in a folder, and obsidian just reads it."

This however, was only the top-most surface area of the architecture; as soon as I made contact with the deep complexity of the systems, I became increasingly cognitively exhausted as finite resources (attention, time, energy, context, continuity etc.)

I am absolutely stunned by how complex the process truly is. And it is merely an extension of a much larger application eco-system. I understand why teams of developers dedicated full workdays to maintaining these systems... the cognitive labor is beyond a single person's capabilities.

The cost of implementation is perhaps one of the sources of energy depletion: Behind the scenes, a simple idea can reveal an enormous underlying clockwork of interconnected systems.

Not only does code exhibit this phenomenon; but information in general.

Among the recent research to develop solutions to this pressing problem, I frequently return to the power of language, as our most natural space for thought and processing. It is the closest we have to translating imagination, into linguistic-code, so-to-speak.
