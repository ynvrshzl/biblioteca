
Yes. There is a surprisingly rich historical vocabulary for what you are trying to name, and some of the strongest candidates are not actually synonyms for “space” in the modern geometric sense. They concern place, disposition, condition, boundary, orientation, order, habitation, and becoming. That distinction matters because your proposed field is not really about “space” alone.

The central conceptual move I would make is this:

> You are trying to model a human being as a temporally changing ecology of internal conditions, situated within an external ecology, and to represent that ecology cartographically.

That is much more precise than “headspace.”

There is also an important piece of friction with your hypothesis. The proposition “two people experience different lives because of unseen internal conditions” is plausible, but it should not become “the external environment is secondary.” Bronfenbrenner's ecological theory explicitly models development as reciprocal interaction between biological characteristics and nested environments, and his later bioecological model places recurring person–environment processes at the center. There is even a published “phenomenological variant” of ecological systems theory specifically attempting to integrate subjective experience with ecological systems. ([APA Dictionary](https://dictionary.apa.org/ecological-systems-theory?utm_source=chatgpt.com "APA Dictionary of Psychology"))

So your eventual model may be strongest if it is not:

**external world → internal psyche**

or

**internal psyche → external behavior**

but:

**person ↔ internal state ↔ lived environment ↔ time → behavior/experience → changed state**

That is an actual dynamical system.

And this is where the vocabulary becomes extremely interesting.

### 1. The Greek word that may be closest to your idea: χώρα — chōra

**χώρα (chōra)** is exceptionally interesting.

The LSJ gives it senses including land, country, region, place, and—quite remarkably—“space or room in which a thing is,” distinguishing it from other Greek concepts of space/place. It can therefore move between geographical territory and the space occupied by something. ([Perseus Atlas](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n115193/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "χώρα — LSJ — Scaife ATLAS"))

That makes **Chora** much more interesting for your project than simply _topos_.

Your idea of an individual having an internally differentiated “territory” could therefore be expressed metaphorically as:

**psychic chōra**  
**inner chōra**  
**biochōra**  
**physiochōra**  
**chronochōra**

The last three would be neologisms, not established scientific terms, but the underlying Greek is legitimate.

There is also an important philosophical lineage here. Plato's _Timaeus_ famously uses **chōra** in a highly unusual sense concerning the “receptacle” or underlying space in which becoming occurs. Aristotle subsequently develops a considerably more concrete theory of **topos**, place. The distinction between _chōra_ and _topos_ is therefore philosophically useful for your project.

I would be cautious about using _Chora_ as the name of the entire field, however. It already carries a substantial philosophical history, and appropriating it would force your terminology to inherit Plato's metaphysical baggage.

But as a foundational concept?

**Extremely strong.**

### 2. τόπος — topos: place

**τόπος (topos)** is the more obvious Greek word.

It means **place, location, position, region**, and became the root of words such as topology and topography.

But Aristotle makes it much more interesting than a simple synonym for “space.” His _Physics_ develops _topos_ as a principle of place, including the idea of the place of a thing as related to the boundary of what contains it. Modern scholarship explicitly treats Aristotle's concept of place in terms of location, boundary, movement, and “being somewhere.” ([OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/6097?utm_source=chatgpt.com "On Location: Aristotle's Concept of Place | Oxford Academic"))

That maps beautifully onto your cartographic intuition.

A **topos** is not merely “an empty space.”

It is:

**where something is.**

That distinction is enormous.

Your question:

> “Why does Amy respond differently to the same workplace situation at 9 AM versus 9 PM?”

could therefore become a question of **topos + chronos + state**.

Not merely:

> Where is Amy?

but:

> What is Amy's current place within her physiological, psychological, temporal, and environmental configuration?

This suggests fascinating compounds:

**Psychotopos** — psychological place  
**Physiotopos** — physiological place  
**Chronotopos** — temporal place  
**Biotopos** — life-place / biological place  
**Intratopos** — internal place

There is an established word **chronotope**, especially in literary theory through Mikhail Bakhtin, meaning a configuration of time and space. That makes _chronotope_ particularly relevant to your project, although you would need to distinguish your usage carefully.

### 3. ἕξις — hexis: state, condition, disposition

This one is perhaps even more important.

**ἕξις (hexis)** is a Greek philosophical and medical term concerning a relatively enduring **state, condition, habit, disposition, or way of being**.

The LSJ specifically records senses including “state or habit of body,” “condition,” and even bodily condition, while contrasting _hexis_ with more alterable conditions. ([Perseus Atlas](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n37405/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "ἕξις — LSJ — Scaife ATLAS"))

Aristotle uses _hexis_ extensively in his account of virtue.

This is extremely close to your distinction between:

> “What state is this person currently in?”

and

> “What kind of stable disposition does this person possess?”

Those are not the same thing.

You could therefore construct a useful hierarchy:

**Topos** = where  
**Chronos** = when  
**Hexis** = condition/disposition  
**Tropos** = manner/mode  
**Telos** = toward what end  
**Peras** = where the boundary lies

That begins looking less like a list of synonyms and more like the beginnings of a formal ontology.

### 4. τρόπος — tropos: mode, manner, direction

**τρόπος (tropos)** comes from the idea of turning.

Its meanings include **turn, direction, course, way, character, manner, style, mode**, and method. ([Perseus Atlas](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n105531/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "τρόπος — LSJ — Scaife ATLAS"))

This is particularly useful for your idea of transitions.

A “state” tells us:

> where the system currently is.

A _tropos_ tells us something about:

> how the system is operating or moving.

That distinction could become important.

For example:

Amy may be in the same broad physiological state on two mornings, but her _tropos_—her mode of responding—could differ.

So:

**Hexis = state/disposition**

**Tropos = mode of operation**

**Metabole = change/transformation**

**Kinesis = movement/change**

This begins to resemble the vocabulary of a dynamical system rather than conventional personality psychology.

### 5. τάξις — taxis: arrangement, order, disposition

This may be one of the hidden gems.

**τάξις (taxis)** means **arrangement, order, disposition, position, rank**, and even “the post or place” occupied by something. The LSJ explicitly records _taxis_ in relation to arrangement and the “disposition” of the soul, as well as temporal ordering. ([Perseus Atlas](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n102381/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "τάξις — LSJ — Scaife ATLAS"))

This is almost tailor-made for your cartographic idea.

You are not merely mapping variables.

You are mapping their **arrangement**.

For example:

Sleep → cortisol → alertness → food → social exposure → work demands → emotional response.

The meaning of each factor depends partly upon its position relative to the others.

That is _taxis_.

And **σύνταξις (syntaxis)** means putting things together in an arrangement or order. ([Perseus Atlas](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n100957/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "σύνταξις — LSJ — Scaife ATLAS"))

This gives you a potentially beautiful conceptual distinction:

**Atlas** = representation of the terrain.

**Taxis** = arrangement of the elements.

**Topos** = location.

**Hexis** = condition.

**Chronos** = temporal dimension.

**Tropos** = mode of operation.

**Telos** = direction/end.

Now we're getting somewhere.

### 6. πέρας — peras: boundary, limit

**πέρας (peras)** is straightforward but powerful:

**boundary, limit, terminus, end.** ([Perseus Atlas](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionary-entries.atlas_v1%3Alexicon-thucydideum-3694/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "πέρας — Lexicon Thucydideum — Scaife ATLAS"))

Your idea depends heavily on transitions between “spaces.”

But a transition requires a boundary.

For example:

normal alertness → fatigue  
fatigue → exhaustion  
solitude → social engagement  
calm → anxiety  
sleep → waking  
hunger → satiation

These aren't necessarily discrete boxes. They can be gradients.

So _peras_ might represent the **threshold** at which one state becomes another.

This leads naturally to:

**Physioperas** — physiological threshold  
**Psychoperas** — psychological boundary  
**Chronoperas** — temporal boundary

Again, these would be constructed terms rather than established scientific vocabulary.

But conceptually they are clean.

### 7. κόσμος — kosmos: ordered world

This is another word I would seriously consider.

**κόσμος (kosmos)** originally concerns **order, arrangement, organization**, and eventually the ordered universe.

There is an especially beautiful precedent here: the LSJ records **κόσμησις (kosmēsis)** as “ordering, arrangement, disposition,” including a passage concerning the **orders/dispositions of the soul** in Plato's _Gorgias_. ([Perseus Atlas](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n59297/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "κόσμησις — LSJ — Scaife ATLAS"))

That is much closer to your project than the modern popular meaning of “cosmos.”

Your internal environment isn't merely a collection of variables.

It has an **order**.

Hence:

**microkosmos** = a small/inner world

is already an ancient conceptual structure.

Your “internal ecology” could therefore be understood as a **microcosm**: a small ordered world whose components interact.

That has substantial historical precedent.

### 8. οἶκος — oikos: household / inhabited environment

Your use of “eco” is also directionally interesting.

**oikos** is the Greek root behind **economy** and **ecology**.

The important idea isn't simply “nature.”

It is **household / dwelling / inhabited domain**.

Ecology literally developed from this notion of the relations of organisms with their environment.

This makes **infraecology** semantically understandable, but I would be careful.

“Infra-” normally means below, beneath, or subordinate. It might accidentally imply an ecological layer beneath ordinary ecology rather than an **internal ecology**.

Something like:

**intraecology**

would linguistically communicate “within,” although it is already adjacent to existing terminology and therefore less distinctive.

You could instead use:

**end(o)-** = within/interior

giving possibilities like:

**endoecology**

or

**endophysiology**

But again, existing scientific uses become important.

### 9. Latin gives you an unexpectedly excellent word: orbis

**orbis** means **circle, sphere, territory, region, world**, and can also refer to a geographical or climatic zone. It can additionally carry meanings associated with rotation, orbit, period, and cycle. ([Latin Dictionary](https://www.latin-dictionary.net/search/latin/orbis?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Latin Definitions for: orbis (Latin Search) - Latin Dictionary and Grammar Resources - Latdict"))

That is almost uncannily aligned with your “atlas + cycles + internal climate” idea.

Consider:

**Orbis vitae**  
“the circle/world of a life”

**Orbis interior**  
“inner sphere/world”

**Orbis physiologicus**  
“physiological sphere”

**Orbis psychicus**  
“psychological sphere”

The attraction of _orbis_ is that it simultaneously suggests:

**world + territory + boundary + cycle + sphere.**

That is unusually dense semantic compression.

And historically, _orbis_ appears in expressions such as **orbis terrarum**, the “world/circle of lands.” ([Online Latin Dictionary](https://www.online-latin-dictionary.com/latin-english-dictionary.php?lemma=ORBIS100&utm_source=chatgpt.com "orbis - Latin to English | Latin Dictionary Olivetti"))

Your atlas metaphor could therefore become an **orbis** rather than merely a “space.”

### 10. “Sphere” is actually better than “dome”

Your intuition about **Physiodome** is understandable, but “dome” introduces a particular architectural geometry.

**Sphere** is conceptually broader.

A sphere implies:

- a bounded but multidimensional domain
    
- an environment
    
- a world
    
- an area of influence
    
- a system surrounding a center
    

So:

**Physiosphere**

is semantically cleaner than **Physiodome**.

But there is a problem: _physiosphere_ already has potential collisions with biological/environmental terminology.

Likewise:

**psychosphere**

already appears in various contexts.

Therefore the word may be conceptually excellent but lexically crowded.

## The really interesting discovery: you may not need one word

Your project seems to contain at least **six different concepts** that are currently being forced into the single metaphor “space.”

I would separate them.

Think of the atlas as having dimensions:

**Topos — place**  
Where am I?

**Chronos — time**  
When am I?

**Hexis — condition**  
What state am I in?

**Tropos — mode**  
How am I operating?

**Taxis — arrangement**  
How are the factors organized?

**Peras — boundary**  
Where does this state cease to be another?

**Telos — direction/end**  
Toward what am I moving?

That is a much stronger conceptual skeleton than “psychological space.”

And now your Aristotle connection becomes considerably more interesting.

You mentioned **Telosphere**.

I would not discard it.

But I would change what it means.

Rather than:

> Telosphere = a space of flourishing

you could define:

> **Telosphere = the directional structure of a life organized toward its ends.**

Then the atlas could describe where the person actually is, while the telos describes where the person is attempting to go.

That creates the distinction between **map** and **orientation**.

A map without orientation merely describes terrain.

A human flourishing model requires orientation.

## Your “internal weather” metaphor is stronger than it initially appears

The weather analogy works because weather has several properties that match your proposed model:

**Weather is dynamic.**

It changes continuously.

**Weather is multidimensional.**

Temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, precipitation, visibility, etc. coexist.

**Weather is partly predictable.**

Circadian and ultradian rhythms may create recurring patterns without determining every individual episode.

**Weather has states.**

Calm, storm, transition, front, drought, etc.

**Weather has thresholds.**

A small change in temperature can cause a phase transition such as condensation.

**Weather interacts with geography.**

The same atmospheric conditions produce different phenomena depending upon terrain.

And critically:

**weather does not equal climate.**

That distinction may be enormously useful for your system.

You could have:

**State** = current weather  
**Rhythm** = recurring cycle  
**Climate** = long-term tendency  
**Terrain** = relatively stable characteristics  
**Environment** = external conditions  
**Event** = perturbation  
**Front** = transition  
**Forecast** = prediction  
**Atlas** = representation  
**Navigation** = intervention

Suddenly your “internal weather clock” becomes something much more rigorous.

## And this gives your hypothesis a more scientifically defensible form

Instead of:

> “The environment is mirrored within the psyche.”

I would formulate the research question more cautiously:

> **How does the interaction between external environment, physiological state, psychological state, temporal position, learned disposition, and behavioral history produce individually distinctive patterns of lived experience?**

That formulation has much less metaphysical baggage.

It also aligns better with existing ecological psychology and bioecological theory. Bronfenbrenner's framework explicitly treats the person as embedded in interacting environmental systems, including biological characteristics and temporal processes. ([Psychology.com](https://psychology.com/concepts/bronfenbrenner-ecological-systems-theory?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory | Psychology.com"))

And your proposed “atlas” is not completely alien to existing psychology. What is unusual is the attempt to make the representation explicitly **dynamic, temporal, phenomenological, physiological, and navigational** rather than merely diagnostic.

That is where your idea potentially becomes interesting.

If we rank them according to semantic usefulness rather than aesthetic appeal:

**Topos** — place  
**Chōra** — inhabited/occupied space, region  
**Hexis** — condition/disposition  
**Tropos** — mode/direction  
**Taxis** — arrangement/order  
**Peras** — boundary/limit  
**Orbis** — sphere/world/region/cycle  
**Telos** — end/purpose/direction  
**Chronos** — time  
**Kosmos** — ordered world  
**Oikos** — inhabited household/environment  
**Psychodex** — excellent invented atlas/instrument name  
**Physioaxis** — excellent structural/coordinate concept  
**Telosphere** — excellent philosophical umbrella concept

And there is a particularly elegant possibility hiding here:

**The Psychodex maps the individual's Orbis through Topos, Chronos, Hexis, Tropos and Taxis toward Telos.**

That sentence is almost a miniature ontology for the entire project.

The **Psychodex** becomes the instrument.

The **Orbis** becomes the person's lived world.

**Topos** describes location/context.

**Chronos** describes temporal position.

**Hexis** describes condition/disposition.

**Tropos** describes mode of functioning.

**Taxis** describes the arrangement of interacting factors.

**Peras** describes thresholds and transitions.

**Telos** provides orientation.

And the **Atlas** is the visual representation of all of it.

That is considerably more powerful than trying to find a single Greek-looking word that means “inner psychological space.”

The deeper historical precedent is also encouraging: Aristotle's vocabulary already refuses to reduce a thing to one isolated property. Place, movement, form, condition, disposition, purpose and change are relational concepts. His theory of _topos_, for example, treats place in relation to what surrounds and contains a thing rather than as simply an empty container. ([DergiPark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/temasa/article/1595075?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Aristoteles’te Yerin Topolojik Gücü - Temasa Journal of Philosophy"))

That gives your project a useful philosophical constraint:

**Do not model the human being as a thing occupying a psychological space. Model the human being as a changing system whose state is constituted through relations among organism, environment, time, history, and action.**

That distinction could be the difference between an evocative metaphor and an actual research program.

And there is already a remarkable bridge toward it in Bronfenbrenner: the established bioecological model treats development as a process involving both biopsychological characteristics and environmental context across time, while phenomenological variants have explicitly attempted to incorporate subjective/interpersonal experience into ecological systems theory. ([APA Dictionary](https://dictionary.apa.org/ecological-systems-theory?utm_source=chatgpt.com "APA Dictionary of Psychology"))

So I would not begin by inventing **Physiodome** or **Sphemonere** yet.

I would first construct the **lexicon of the ontology**:

**place → state → mode → time → boundary → transition → arrangement → environment → trajectory → telos**

Then name the field after we know exactly what the ontology contains.

That would also make the eventual title genuinely etymological rather than merely _sounding_ scientific—which is exactly the distinction you are reaching for.
