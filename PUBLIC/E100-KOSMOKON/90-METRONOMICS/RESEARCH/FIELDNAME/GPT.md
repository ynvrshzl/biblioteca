Yes. After reading your draft, I would actually move away from “heuristic” as the primary root. Your paper is not fundamentally about rules of thumb or problem-solving heuristics. The central operation you describe is much closer to “finding a usable measure by establishing relations between things that cannot be measured precisely.” That distinction is important. Your own formulation is essentially “proportion over precision.”

There is also a surprisingly strong historical vocabulary for this.

The most promising Greek root is μέτρον (_metron_).

In Greek, μέτρον is not merely “a number.” It means a measure, measuring standard, limit, or extent. Aristotle explicitly treated measurement as a philosophical problem, including the question of how different kinds of quantities become measurable through appropriate units. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/aristotle-mathematics/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Aristotle and Mathematics (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy/Spring 2026 Edition)")) This makes _metron_ much closer to your idea than _arithmos_ (“number”) is.

Even more interesting is συμμετρία (_symmetria_): literally something like “having a common measure.” It is formed from σύν (_syn_, together) + μέτρον (_metron_, measure). The ancient concept therefore concerns the relationship of parts through a shared measure rather than merely assigning independent numerical values. ([ΛΟΓΟΣ](https://lexarithmos.app/en/lexicon/symmetriakos/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "ΣΥΜΜΕΤΡΙΚΟΝ (συμμετρικόν), definition and etymology | LOGOS"))

That gives us a potentially beautiful distinction:

> **Arithmetic:** determining quantity by number.  
> **Metrology:** determining quantity by standardized measure.  
> **Symmetria:** determining relationship through common measure.  
> **Analogia:** determining relationship through proportion.

And your proposed discipline seems to sit particularly close to the last two.

### 1. Analogia — ἀναλογία

This may actually be your strongest candidate.

ἀναλογία (_analogia_) means “proportion,” “correspondence,” or “analogy.” Its important feature is that it doesn't require the objects themselves to possess identical units. It establishes a relation between relations. ([Bible Hub](https://biblehub.com/strongs/greek/356.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Strong's Greek: 356. ἀναλογία (analogia) -- Proportion, analogy"))

That is extraordinarily close to what you're attempting.

Suppose we want to evaluate:

> “How useful is this piece of information for achieving goal X?”

There may be no natural unit called a “usefulness.” But you can establish relations:

> information A is more useful than B  
> B is approximately twice as useful as C  
> A is highly relevant relative to goal X  
> information D is low relevance but high reliability

The measurement emerges from **relations**, rather than from an intrinsic numerical property.

That is very much _analogía_.

You could therefore construct:

**Analogics** — the study of proportional relations between heterogeneous or qualitatively different things.

Or, if you want the mathematical flavor:

**Analogic mathematics**

**Analogical mathematics**

**Analogia** as the name of the underlying principle.

There is historical precedent for this being more than poetic terminology. Greek mathematical and philosophical traditions used proportion and analogy to establish correspondence between otherwise different magnitudes and domains. Aristotle's _Topics_ and _Rhetoric_, for example, deal with analogical reasoning when demonstrative certainty is unavailable. ([ΛΟΓΟΣ](https://lexarithmos.app/en/lexicon/analogismos/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "ΑΝΑΛΟΓΙΣΜΟΣ (ἀναλογισμός), definition and etymology | LOGOS"))

### 2. Symmetria — συμμετρία

This is perhaps even more philosophically interesting.

The modern word “symmetry” has narrowed considerably. We usually imagine mirror symmetry.

But the Greek concept συμμετρία is much closer to **commensurability, proportion, and harmonious relation of parts**. Its underlying idea is literally “common measure.” ([ΛΟΓΟΣ](https://lexarithmos.app/en/lexicon/symmetria/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "ΣΥΜΜΕΤΡΙΑ (συμμετρία), definition and etymology | LOGOS"))

That gives you an intriguing conceptual formulation:

> **Heurithmetics:** measuring what cannot be precisely measured.  
> **Symmetria:** establishing a common measure between things.

And that latter formulation may be closer to your actual project.

For example:

> “High information quality relative to the goal.”

You are searching for a **common scale** upon which “information,” “quality,” and “goal relevance” can become relationally comparable.

That is essentially a search for _symmetria_.

One possible name would therefore be:

**Symmetrics**

or

**Symmetrology**

although the latter risks colliding conceptually with existing metrology.

### 3. Metron — μέτρον

If you want the simplest and deepest root, I would seriously consider building the terminology around _metron_.

Your project could be:

**Metronics** — the study of measurement.

But unfortunately, “metronics” already has technical associations in modern engineering, so it wouldn't give your discipline a clean identity.

You could instead use:

**Metretics**

from Greek μετρητής (_metrētēs_), “measurer.”

Or:

**Qualimetronics**

though that starts becoming modern/constructed terminology rather than genuinely Greek.

There is another possibility that I find conceptually stronger:

**Metriology**

—not as a claim that this is an established historical Greek word, but as a deliberately constructed modern term from _metron_ + _logos_: “reasoned discourse/study of measure.”

That would parallel your idea of a systematic language for measurement.

### 4. Analogismos — ἀναλογισμός

This is particularly interesting because it gets very close to your original “Heurithmetics.”

ἀναλογισμός (_analogismos_) is associated with reasoning through proportional relationships. Historical Greek usage connects it with reckoning, proportional reasoning, and inference where complete demonstrative knowledge is unavailable. ([ΛΟΓΟΣ](https://lexarithmos.app/en/lexicon/analogismos/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "ΑΝΑΛΟΓΙΣΜΟΣ (ἀναλογισμός), definition and etymology | LOGOS"))

So you could have:

**Arithmetic** → calculation through number.

**Analogismos** → calculation/reasoning through proportion.

That is almost exactly the conceptual distinction you were reaching for with “Heurithmetics.”

The problem is that _analogismos_ historically carries a stronger sense of **reasoning/inference** than measurement itself. So it captures the _method_ but not necessarily the _object_ of your proposed discipline.

### 5. Latin modus

Latin offers a surprisingly beautiful candidate: **modus**.

_Modus_ means “measure,” “limit,” “extent,” “manner,” “method,” or “way.” The Oxford Latin Dictionary also gives senses involving measured amount, unit of measurement, size, extent, and proportion. ([Ancient Languages](https://ancientlanguages.org/latin/dictionary/modus-modi?utm_source=chatgpt.com "modus (Latin noun) - \"measure\" - Allo Latin"))

This creates an interesting conceptual bridge:

> **modus = measure → limit → manner → mode**

In other words, a “mode” can be understood historically as a **way determined by a measure**.

Potential constructions:

**Modistics**

**Modulistics**

**Modular mathematics**

Although again, many of these already have modern technical meanings.

### 6. Hebrew middah — מִדָּה

Hebrew provides an unexpectedly good candidate.

מִדָּה (_middāh_) means “measure,” “measurement,” “size,” “stature,” “extent,” and “measured portion.” Its semantic field therefore isn't restricted to a numerical measurement; it can refer to the **extent or size of something**. ([Bible Hub](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/4060.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Strong's Hebrew: 4060. מִדָּה (middah) -- Measure, measurement, stature, size"))

The underlying Hebrew verb מדד (_mādad_) means essentially “to measure,” with the concrete sense of stretching, extending, or spreading something out. ([Bible Tools](https://www.bibletools.org/index.cfm/fuseaction/Def.show/RTD/ISBE/ID/5883/Measure-Measures.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Measure; Measures (International Standard Bible Encyclopedia)"))

This is interesting for your project because your proposed operation isn't necessarily:

> “Assign X the numerical value 7.”

It is more like:

> “Determine the extent of X relative to a relevant field.”

_Middah_ therefore has a conceptual flavor of **extent / measure / proportion** rather than purely numerical calculation.

A constructed term such as **Middahics** would be historically evocative, although I would hesitate to use a Hebrew word merely because it sounds exotic. If the paper isn't substantially grounded in Hebrew thought, Greek gives you a cleaner intellectual lineage.

There is another word that I think deserves special attention: **kairos** — καιρός.

It isn't “measurement” in the ordinary sense. Rather, _kairos_ concerns the **fitting, appropriate, opportune, or decisive moment**, in contrast with _chronos_, quantitative/continuous time. Classical Greek discussions connect it with appropriateness, proportion, and the particular circumstances of an action. ([ΛΟΓΟΣ](https://lexarithmos.app/en/lexicon/kairos/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "ΚΑΙΡΟΣ (καιρός), definition and etymology | LOGOS"))

This is extremely relevant to your example of information.

Imagine two statements:

> “This information is objectively true.”

versus

> “This information is valuable **for this goal, at this time, under these circumstances**.”

The second is fundamentally contextual.

A fact can be perfectly accurate but useless **now**. Another fact can be approximate but enormously useful **now**.

That is a _kairos_-like dimension of value.

So perhaps your system has two axes:

**Metron** — how much?

**Kairos** — how appropriate/useful here and now?

And then:

**Analogia** — how does that quantity/quality compare to something else?

That starts looking less like an arbitrary invented mathematics and more like a potential conceptual framework.

### Where “Phenomenological Mathematics” fits

I actually think your phrase **phenomenological mathematics** is intellectually defensible, but it describes the _source of the measurements_ rather than the mathematical operation itself.

Your draft says:

> “Heurithmetics studies how we measure naturally as human beings, using proportion and scale to measure real-world artifacts, or non-precise objects.”

That is phenomenological in an important sense: you're beginning with **how something is experienced or encountered**, rather than assuming that the phenomenon arrives already equipped with numerical coordinates.

But phenomenology traditionally concerns the structures of experience and consciousness; it isn't itself a theory of approximate measurement. So “phenomenological mathematics” could easily be interpreted as mathematics _about consciousness_, which isn't quite your proposal.

Your idea seems narrower:

> **How can an observer establish useful measures for phenomena whose relevant properties do not possess naturally precise numerical units?**

That is a much more interesting and technically testable question.

And there is a very important historical friction here: **not everything qualitative can necessarily be converted into a legitimate quantitative scale.**

This is where your project will need to be disciplined.

“Information quality” isn't automatically measurable merely because humans can rank things as better/worse. You need to establish what kind of measurement relation exists. Does “twice as useful” actually mean anything? Is the scale ordinal, interval, ratio, or merely comparative? Can different observers agree? Does the measurement remain stable when the goal changes?

These are not minor objections. They are the central mathematical problem.

In fact, your own observation that context changes the meaning of “high” already points directly toward it: “high” may not be experienced equally by two individuals.

That means your system probably shouldn't initially claim:

> **“We can mathematically measure qualitative things.”**

A more rigorous hypothesis would be:

> **“Some qualitative phenomena can be represented by structured comparative, proportional, or relational measures even when they cannot be represented by precise cardinal quantities.”**

That is a much stronger proposition.

And interestingly, this brings you very close to an established mathematical distinction between **ordinal**, **interval**, and **ratio** measurement. Your proposed mathematics may not need to invent measurement from nothing; it may be exploring how far measurement theory can operate when the underlying objects are contextual, qualitative, heterogeneous, or only partially commensurable.

### My current ranking

If the goal is to name the _discipline_:

**1. Analogia / Analogical Mathematics**  
Best for: proportion, correspondence, relational measurement.

**2. Metron / Metretics**  
Best for: measurement itself, especially measurement without insisting upon numerical precision.

**3. Symmetria**  
Best for: finding common measures between heterogeneous things.

**4. Phenomenological Mathematics**  
Best for: emphasizing that the measurements originate in human experience/encounter.

**5. Analogismos**  
Best for: proportional reasoning when exact demonstration is unavailable.

**6. Heurithmetics**  
Best as a deliberately invented name, but semantically it emphasizes _heuristic discovery_ more than _measurement by proportion_.

There is an especially elegant possibility here:

> **Heurithmetics could be the methodology.**  
> **Analogia could be the mathematical principle.**  
> **Metron could be the concept of measure.**  
> **Symmetria could be the establishment of a common scale.**

Then the project doesn't need one word to carry the entire philosophical burden.

You could formulate the underlying proposition almost as an ancient Greek mathematical aphorism:

> **“Measure need not begin with number; it may begin with relation.”**

That would also connect very naturally to your existing “Proportion over precision” principle.

And there is a particularly promising research direction here: **the history of proportional reasoning from Pythagorean mathematics → Plato → Aristotle → Euclid → medieval ratio theory → modern measurement theory → psychophysics → information theory → contemporary decision theory.** That lineage could tell us whether your “non-numerical mathematics” is genuinely a new category, or whether you are rediscovering an older mathematical idea that modern mathematics subsequently formalized differently.
