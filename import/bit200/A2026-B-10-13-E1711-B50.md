1, Optimization timespace hypothesis: "We are not limited by space but by time." 

This propostiion has fascinatingly deep implications, not only in Cyberneering, but in general life.

"What if there are indeed optimal (or more optimal) methods of approaching various situations or systems in Human life. Those optimal (or near-optimal) potentialites are essentially distributions in a space of general combinatorial-potentiality. Fascinatingly, the space of usefullness (or general structural integrity) are naturally concentrated towards an attractor (or gravity source). However, we do not know the scale nor geometry of this space, in order ot calculate such optimialty. Therefore, We must sample this shared space where Entropy and Order co-exist (in a Continuity or Continuum). What if however, we are not limited by space, but by time? If we cannot possibly explore this potentiality-space versus-actuality versus phronesis... then does this naturally leave us in a postiion of stability over specialization? Meaning, if a sophisticated system is arrange in less-than-optimal state; does this inherently mean the designer has not optimized properly? Or is this a natural law of all systems, whether Human-authored or otherwise?"

5, Language design theory: For our computer-science lexical Cybernering projects, (grammatica, scehatma, etc.), plaintext code can only represent so much tangibility within text. CLIs and Terminal-based applications use the graphical nature of certian ASCII characters to build graphical representations and enviornments. However, this has serious limitations (the question here is whether or not the system is working against the graphical necessity...). 

Graphics are ambigious but tangible, while instruction and concepts are abstract without tangibility. 

This is the reason why we don't communicate in Egyptian Hieroglyphics; graphics are inherently amgigious and require communication in some fundamental sense. Perhaps code itself should maxmize source of representation (instruction and category), rather than merely trying to represent the output-as-data. Lisp is a fascinating example of this concept; where data and code are the same shared-space.
