# Analysis of mthematics and architecture of language

How are mathematical functions constructed, using pure mathematics? Does this reveal a fundamental incompleteness of mathematics? Or is the question more of philosophical ontology, rather than practicality.

For example, by using lower-level mathematics, a function may be desribed as...

$$
f = (a, x, b)
$$

Where the function itself is the expression of a co-ordinate pair of parameters "a" and "b" and the "x" is the transformation by which the function operates. Note, that this is a very rough draft of a working model I am developing, but in essence, a "summation" function could take leading arguments, and evaluate by an operator, eliminating the arithmetic notation, in favor of a transformation interface;

$$
f = (a, x, b), sum = f(a + b), sum(32, 16) = 4
$$

This starts to resemble code transformation... which is honestly fascinating becuase we move from calculation to computation... and much of the modern civilization is powered by these simple micro-machines of transformation, assembled across billions of little transistors and gates...

But perhaps I am getting ahead of myself here; I am not sure if this is hyperbolic mathematics, or if it is accurate whatsoever to the technical mechanics of the language, but it is fascinating to see how math emerges language-like grammar and ideas; expressibility emerging from the symbol, the rule and the constraint. That's a deeper line of inquiry alltogether.

This converges on an interesting idea; the power of mathematics is not in the abstraction of symbols; algebra makes no attempt to occlude the fact. Mathematics is powerful becuase of it's language-like features; not becuase symbols are perfect axioms, discoveries or arbitrary inventions; but becuase the symbols are stable enough to map onto real ideas, concepts, definitions and rules, authored observed, tested and verified by the human being.

So then, this convrges into a deeper vein of inquiry; the architecture of language. My very brief, crude understanding of this is as follows: a b, c, d, e.

The general lifecycle of the construction of a language: "a" is the problem, observation or phenomena. "b" is an idea, rule, regulation... "c" is the symbolic representation... "d" is the abstraction or assembly stage of symbolic representation, and "e" is the evaluation stage, which is something of a cybernetic feedback loop. This stage asks "are we modelling the problem accurately?" And even more critically: "Are we modelling the correct problem? Are we modelling this chunk of the natural world, accurately? Enough to be understood?" (...still editing this part.)

In this sense, language is deeply semiotic; fundamentally based on real phenomena, but transcribed as abstractions of symbols. This, appears to be out of necessity. Even words themselves, in sentences, must be hierarchically compressed; the search space and density of information would otherwise overload our capacities to process information.

In conclusion, nothing in mathematics is self-created. Even arithmetic comes from a real-world observation; not a vacuum. This is crucial becuase if something as mechanically and logiclly pure as mathematics, requires a system outside of itself; then perhaps this proposes far deeper implications about meaning in human life...
