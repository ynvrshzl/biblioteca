From what is available, your "Hemispheres" hypothesis was **not primarily about left-brain/right-brain neuroscience**. It was closer to a theory of **human experience, cognition, and information architecture**.

The major pieces I can recover are:

### 1. The Internal-World Problem

One formulation you explored was:

> Why can two people occupy the same external reality, yet inhabit radically different experiential realities?

This led toward a distinction between:

- **External world** (events, objects, environment)
    
- **Internal world** (interpretations, memories, emotions, concepts)
    
- **Process layer** (the mechanisms transforming one into the other)
    

You were interested in how different "hemispheres" of experience emerge even when the external inputs appear similar.

Historically, this resembles questions asked by:

- William James regarding streams of consciousness.
    
- Immanuel Kant regarding the distinction between the world as experienced and the world in itself.
    
- Jakob von Uexküll and his concept of an organism's _Umwelt_—each creature inhabiting a different perceptual world despite sharing the same physical environment.
    

---

### 2. Connection to the Youniverse Model

The hypothesis later became linked to your broader **Youniverse** framework.

The idea was that each person contains an entire internal landscape:

- memories
    
- beliefs
    
- symbolic structures
    
- emotional territories
    
- conceptual maps
    

In that framing, "hemispheres" seemed less like anatomical structures and more like **regions of a cognitive world**.

---

### 3. Spatial Cognition and Navigation

A later development connected hemispheres to your LifeOS research.

You were exploring observations such as:

- dedicated spaces influence thought
    
- objects become cognitive anchors
    
- context affects memory retrieval
    
- navigation affects reasoning
    

Examples included:

- a piano room encouraging music-related thinking
    
- a workshop encouraging engineering thinking
    
- a code lab encouraging programming thinking
    

The core insight appeared to be:

> Human cognition may be fundamentally navigational.

Rather than merely storing facts, humans move through conceptual terrain.

This resembles ideas from:

- Edward Tolman and cognitive maps.
    
- Christopher Alexander and pattern-based environments.
    
- Vannevar Bush whose Memex envisioned knowledge as trails rather than files.
    

---

### 4. The "Single Cursor" Observation

One associated idea I found was your concern that humans appear to possess something like a:

> single-cursor model of cognition

Meaning:

- attention moves through one path at a time,
    
- complexity grows faster than attentional capacity,
    
- therefore tools must help externalize structure.
    

In that context, hemispheres may have been serving as **large-scale partitions of complexity** rather than neurological claims.

> Reality is too large for a finite human mind.

> Different people inhabit different internal worlds despite sharing external conditions.
> 
> Spatial organization, context, symbols, and tools shape these worlds.
> 
> "Hemispheres" may be large-scale divisions within these worlds that help finite minds manage infinite complexity.
