Yes. There is a real academic territory here—but your observation becomes more interesting once we distinguish several things that universities currently keep separate.

The short answer is: **“the study of writing” exists; “the general science/craft of authoring” does not exist as a single, unified discipline.** The fragmentation you are noticing is real.

There are at least four established academic traditions surrounding your proposed subject:

- **Linguistics** studies language as a system.
    
- **Rhetoric and composition** studies how people construct texts and communicate through them.
    
- **Writing Studies** studies the production, circulation, uses, technologies, and effects of writing.
    
- **Creative Writing / Creative Writing Studies** studies the practice and pedagogy of producing literary works.
    
- **Literary studies** primarily studies existing works—their forms, histories, meanings, contexts, and effects.
    

For example, the University of California, Santa Barbara explicitly defines Writing Studies as research into the “production, consumption, and circulation of writing,” encompassing composition and rhetoric, computers and writing, genre studies, second-language writing, and textual analysis. ([Comparative Literature Program](https://www.complit.ucsb.edu/graduate/graduate-program-emphases/writing-studies-emphasis?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Writing Studies Emphasis | Comparative Literature Program")) The University of Utah has an actual Department of Writing & Rhetoric Studies whose mission includes studying the production, circulation, uses, and effects of texts. ([University of Utah Catalog](https://catalog.utah.edu/pages/department-WRTG?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Department of Writing and Rhetoric Studies | University of Utah Catalog"))

So your intuition is not discovering an academic void so much as discovering a **missing synthesis**.

And that distinction is important.

### The missing object may be the Author

Consider what happens institutionally.

A linguist asks:

> What is a sentence?

A literary scholar asks:

> What does this sentence mean within this work and its historical context?

A rhetorician asks:

> What does this sentence do to an audience?

A composition scholar asks:

> How does a person learn to produce this kind of text?

A creative-writing instructor asks:

> How can a writer make this scene more effective?

A cognitive scientist might ask:

> What happens in the mind while composing or reading it?

A narratologist asks:

> How does this narrative structure information?

These are all legitimate questions.

But your proposed discipline asks something slightly different:

> **Given the available resources of language, cognition, rhetoric, narrative, genre, form, perspective, structure, and technology, how does a human being systematically construct an authored artifact?**

That is a much more synthetic question.

It puts **authoring itself** at the center.

And that is surprisingly close to what you are describing as an “Author's Framework.”

### There is even historical evidence that the academy has struggled with exactly this problem

A 2009 doctoral dissertation by Dianne J. Donnelly is extraordinarily relevant. Its title is literally _Establishing Creative Writing Studies as an Academic Discipline_. Donnelly argues that creative writing had historically been relatively undertheorized, heavily dependent on the workshop model, and fragmented in its disciplinary identity. She explicitly argues that Creative Writing Studies should develop its own theory, history, pedagogy, and scholarly identity alongside composition and literary studies. ([Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/etd/3809/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "\"Establishing Creative Writing Studies as an Academic Discipline\" by Dianne J. Donnelly"))

And this wasn't merely an isolated complaint.

Research on creative-writing pedagogy has repeatedly questioned the assumption that writing is primarily something one simply learns by doing. A 2016 University of Michigan dissertation notes longstanding calls for discipline-specific pedagogical training because graduate creative-writing programs historically gave relatively little formal preparation in **how creative writing itself should be taught**. ([Deep Blue](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/1a437e36-8710-4984-9b95-cf212a90c82c?utm_source=chatgpt.com "The Education of the Creative Writing Teacher: A Study of Conceptions of Creative Writing Pedagogy in Higher Education."))

Falmouth University currently describes creative-writing pedagogy as moving from a tradition-guided field toward one increasingly shaped by systematic inquiry into the “how” and “why” of teaching creative writing. ([Falmouth University](https://www.falmouth.ac.uk/research/phd-mphil/doctoral-project-briefs/pedagogy-creative-writing?utm_source=chatgpt.com "The Pedagogy of Creative Writing | Falmouth University"))

So there is a fascinating historical trajectory:

**writing was practiced → writing was taught → writing was studied → writing pedagogy was studied → writing itself became theorized.**

But the synthesis remains incomplete.

Writing isn't merely:

Writing therefore becomes an **external cognitive feedback system**.

This isn't merely speculative. Writing-studies scholarship already treats writing as an iterative process that facilitates thinking, reflection, inquiry, critique, and deliberation, rather than merely transmitting thoughts that already exist. The University of Minnesota's Writing Studies description explicitly makes this argument. ### The really interesting part is that “literary devices” are only the beginning

Imagine teaching writing the way mathematics is taught.

A mathematics curriculum does not merely say:

> Here are some famous mathematical works. Try doing mathematics.

It establishes an expandable vocabulary:

**number → operation → relation → function → structure → proof → model**

Likewise, an Authoring curriculum could establish:

**lexeme → phrase → clause → sentence → paragraph → scene → sequence → work**

But then introduce another dimension:

**description → exposition → dialogue → narration → argument → metaphor → analogy → symbolism → irony → juxtaposition → foreshadowing**

And another:

**first person → second person → third person → omniscience → limited perspective → unreliable narration → free indirect discourse**

And another:

**character → desire → obstacle → decision → action → consequence → change**

And another:

**information → concealment → revelation → anticipation → surprise → reinterpretation**

And another:

**author → narrator → character → reader**

Suddenly literary devices stop being an enormous unordered vocabulary list.

They become components in a **generative system**.

That distinction is enormous.

A student currently encounters “foreshadowing,” “dramatic irony,” “metaphor,” “unreliable narrator,” etc., often as separate concepts.

Your framework would ask:

> **What problem does each device solve in the author's construction of an experience for the reader?**

Foreshadowing becomes an information-management operation.

Dramatic irony becomes an asymmetric-knowledge structure.

Metaphor becomes a cross-domain mapping operation.

Dialogue becomes a mechanism for representing agents interacting through language.

Point of view becomes an information-access constraint.

Scene construction becomes temporal-spatial simulation.

Revision becomes optimization under constraints.

Genre becomes a learned system of expectations.

That is much closer to an **engineering theory of authoring**.

### And this explains why “Authoring” is harder to institutionalize than mathematics

Mathematics has extraordinarily stable formal objects.

Writing does not.

The object being constructed changes depending upon whether you're writing:

a poem,  
a novel,  
a scientific paper,  
a legal contract,  
a screenplay,  
a software specification,  
a newspaper article,  
a diary,  
a philosophical argument,  
a technical manual,  
a comic,  
a speech.

There is no single universally accepted objective function.

A mathematical proof can be evaluated largely through formal validity.

A novel cannot.

Consequently, the discipline would have to separate **general authoring primitives** from **domain-specific authoring systems**.

Something like:

> **General Authoring Theory**
> 
> ↓
> 
> Linguistic Authoring  
> Narrative Authoring  
> Argumentative Authoring  
> Expository Authoring  
> Technical Authoring  
> Poetic Authoring  
> Dramatic Authoring  
> Visual/Multimodal Authoring  
> Interactive/Digital Authoring

That begins to look much more like an actual academic discipline.

And there are already institutional fragments of exactly this architecture. Syracuse, for example, describes Writing and Rhetoric as studying language across genres, rhetorical traditions, new writing technologies, storytelling, identity, literacy, and power. ([Syracuse Arts & Sciences](https://artsandsciences.syracuse.edu/writing-studies-rhetoric-and-composition/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Writing Studies, Rhetoric and Composition - College of Arts & Sciences at Syracuse University"))

### The historical irony is that the ancient Greeks already had part of this

The closest ancient precedent may be **rhetoric**.

Aristotle did not simply catalogue beautiful sentences. In _Rhetoric_, he attempted to systematically analyze how discourse persuades.

Likewise, _Poetics_ attempted to analyze the construction of tragedy.

That is remarkably close to your intuition:

> **Don't merely study stories. Study the machinery by which stories are constructed.**

But modern academia subsequently divided the machinery among disciplines.

Linguistics took language.

Literary studies took texts.

Rhetoric took persuasion.

Psychology took cognition.

Narratology took narrative structure.

Semiotics took signs.

Composition took writing processes.

Creative writing took practice.

Communication studies took media.

Computer science took computational language.

Cognitive science took aspects of reading and composition.

The pieces exist.

**The authoring system is distributed across them.**

That may be the phenomenon you are detecting.

### And there is a second problem: academia tends to study the artifact rather than the craftsperson

There is an asymmetry:

**Literary Studies**

> Work → interpretation

**Linguistics**

> Language → structure

**Rhetoric**

> Discourse → effect

**Writing Studies**

> Writing → process/social context

**Creative Writing**

> Writer → practice → work

Your proposed field would put the entire causal chain under examination:

> **World → Author → Cognition → Intention → Language → Composition → Artifact → Reader → Interpretation → Feedback → Author**

That is a very different unit of analysis.

It is not quite “literature.”

It is not quite “linguistics.”

It is not quite “composition.”

It is not quite “creative writing.”

It is **authoring**.

And that term is actually useful because “writing” describes the medium, whereas **authoring describes the intentional act of constructing an artifact through that medium.**

### Your cybernetic formulation could therefore become much stronger

Instead of defining the discipline as:

> “the study of writing”

I would tentatively define it as:

> **Authoring Studies: the systematic study of how humans conceive, construct, transform, and evaluate communicative artifacts through language and other symbolic media.**

Then its central object is not “the book.”

It is the **authoring process**.

And its fundamental question becomes:

> **How does an author transform intention into an artifact capable of producing effects in another mind?**

That immediately creates a research program.

You could investigate the author's **arsenal**:

lexicon, syntax, grammar, rhetoric, narrative, characterization, perspective, metaphor, symbolism, rhythm, structure, genre, argumentation, information control, dialogue, exposition, description, pacing, revision, editing, typography, multimodality, and eventually interactive media.

Then you could investigate the **authoring process**:

conception → planning → drafting → composition → interruption → revision → criticism → restructuring → publication → reception.

Then the **author-reader system**:

author → artifact → reader → interpretation → feedback.

And finally the **cybernetic loop**:

**Authoring changes the author's cognition, which changes subsequent authoring.**

That last proposition may be one of the deepest parts of your project.

Writing is not only an output channel.

It is also a **cognitive instrument**.

The writer constructs the external representation, encounters the representation as an object, discovers relationships that were not consciously available beforehand, and then reconstructs the representation.

The artifact therefore becomes part of the author's cognitive environment.

This is very close to the broader Writing Studies claim that writing can constitute rather than merely communicate knowledge. ([Writing Across the Curriculum](https://wac.umn.edu/writing-enriched-curriculum/academic-units/writing-studies?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Writing Studies | Writing Across the Curriculum"))

So I would add one important piece of friction to your hypothesis:

**The discipline you are imagining does not need to be invented from nothing.**

That would actually weaken the project.

The more defensible claim is:

> **The intellectual components of Authoring Studies already exist, but they are institutionally and theoretically fragmented across linguistics, rhetoric, composition, writing studies, narratology, literary studies, creative writing, cognitive science, and communication. What appears underdeveloped is a unified framework whose primary object is the authoring process itself.**

That is a much stronger research thesis.

And it gives your “Author's Framework” a very concrete first objective:

**Don't invent another list of literary devices. Build the periodic table of authoring.**

Not literally a periodic table, of course—but a systematic ontology showing what the fundamental operations are, what they operate upon, what transformations they produce, what constraints they satisfy, what cognitive/rhetorical function they serve, and how they compose into larger structures.

That could turn your intuition from “there should be a school of writing” into something academically testable:

**What are the irreducible operations of authoring, and can they be organized into a coherent general theory?**

That is a considerably more formidable—and more interesting—question.
