I have a theory; let's review it for strengths, weaknesses and experience from real authors who wrote real books. The proposition involves "references" as the "essential source of stories." A reference in this case, may be a source of inspiration, i.e., a real-world.

A supporting source; J.R.R Tolkien. In one of his late interviews, he was asked something resembling "What compels you to build worlds and create stories?" Tolkien looks away from the interviewer, off into the distance, and shrugs. What is fascinating is how this is a gesture of humility, following his proceeding response: "I don't know what compels a man to do something like that... we (all) have an innate desire to create... but since we don't (truly) create anything, we must re-arrange things..."

That's fascinating. Tolkien was the author of one of, if not, *the* biggest fantasy novel world ever published. He created entire languages for these stories, and he built maps to support these seemingly "Imaginary" or "Fantasy" worlds.

The reader may question: "How did he do that? Was it maladaptive daydreaming? Was he a wizard? (plausibly.) I suggest "references" and "inspiration" were the elementary principles of Tolkiens stories; and even beyond the published work; his "Cartography of Imagination." Many of his stories were incomplete, re-written or unpublished.

Tolkien is one such examplar of this miraculous ability to "create" things. Authoring is perhaps one of the most native-to-source fields for this study; as authoring is largely dependent on the imagination of both the author and the reader.

Henceforth; imagination appears to play a central role, as a form of "incubation ecosystem" for creativity.

I believe the implications of this study are manifold and deeply relevant to the creative process; most especially in the age of "AI oversaturated" media we are living in today; Creativity is more significant than ever.

This is where the theory unfolds into two parts; Reference and Creation. For context; As Tolkien said, we do not necessarily "create" new things, in the sense of a Divine "Logos." We do however, re-arrange parts in new ways. While we do not sprout new forms of matter into the world; we discover new ways of transformation; e.g., our ability to form connections between loose parts, to unify or divide materials, ultimately forms of "transformation" or "re-arranging" of parts.
