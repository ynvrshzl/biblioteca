Context: I am studying Phenomenological Equillibria; the balance between Chaos and Order, between Complexity and Simplicity, between Sustainability and Entropy; as fundamental properties of all systems, machine or living. I believe the "Phenomenological Equillibria" is perhaps one of the fundamentals of Human-Flourishing and effective systems in general.

This is something like a propositional "Thermodynamics-equivalent for Laws of General Systems." This is especially relevant to help us study and understand how we can manage and implement the Equillibria in the everyday world. From Complexity to Entropy to Time-Management, the implications of these studies are manifold and fascinating; revealing a hidden order of all things.

It may be useful to define the invariants before proceeding, since Phenomenology, and especially, Human-Flourishing is a complex abstract, but I suspect it may be concretiz-able into argument form, and eventual implementation of these ideas, as the Telos.

Speaking of Telos, it appears that systems require fundmamental laws to avoid being consumed by Entropy, while dynamically changing according to enviornmental and internal factors. Of these laws, I propose two fundamental laws that govern sustainable systems; Telos and Embededness.

Telos is an ultimate direction or the purpose of systems; an orientation of aim. This is incredibly significant, in an otherwise, nonfinite space of potential and Entropy, Orientation both reduces search-space, and establishes a structure for the system. 

Embededdness states that a system must be embedded in a net larger than itself; no system can exist in isolation. This is why Mathematics cannot explain itself (Citation: Godel's Theorems of Incompleteness.) It is also why music is deeply meaningful; it integrates rhythm and emotion; the emotion exists nowhere in the Physics of sound, yet Music remains a Universal language, unifying despite Cultural differences.

This is where the third Law emerges; Unifying...

My proposed hypothetical defintion of 'System' is: as structured as unfolding steps. 

The first invariant of a system is 'Manifold' parts; A system requires an interlinked tapestry of parts. The second invariant is process; The parts are dependent on communication. Communication requires , process information. of processes which emerge a whole, larger than the sum of individual parts. In this sense, Aristotle's 'Synergy' is deeply orthogonal to the nature of systems; All things dynamic, living, machinistic, or otherwise composed of processes, may be defined as a 'System.'

A system is therefore, something approximating an emergent structure, rather than a bottom-up predicted architecture.

## Research
What are the most complex fields, subjects and professions today? More importantly; how are they managed, organized and standardized to avoid "information-chaos-overload?" Even more interestingly, has there ever occured, within human history, information which became so complex or convoluted; it led to mis-communication, or even catastrophe? 

I find this preposterously fascinating and worringly complex; some of the hardest work I know of, is not Mathemtics, Physics or Software-Engineering; it is fundamentally the Architecture of Information and Entropy management. 

My proposition with this study is one originating from local Phenomenology; Managing folders, files and computer interfaces, is truly an issue of information architecture. This is significantly more Phenomenologically demanding of finite human-cognitive-resources, than learning structured subjects (Mathematics, Physics, Engineering); regardless of their complexity. This may be due to how we categorize, classify and organize information; and that is a fundamentally Meta-Cognitive ability. It is closer to the "Engineering of Information and Systems" than "Managing filenames." 

A great exercise in effective administration skills is trying to organize a folder with 100s of scattered files; I argue this activity rests at a low-level strata; closer to the nature of information and systems management.

Combinatorically, there are a near infinite number of arrangements for information sytsems, even in a small sample space with a few dozen parts. Filenames, schemas, templates, and coding, all increase system potential, and exhaust human cognitive resources; essentialy "Entropy." Currently, I propose a system requires embeddedness and integration within a larger net than itself, in order to gauge effective search-space.

The Ontology is emerging into something approximating... 1: Telos, 2: Constraints, 3: Implementation, 4: Analysis, 5: Feedback.

For example: constraining file-naming-cases to only kebab-casing or implementing a numeric-indexing system. Or constraing files to use strict template-schemas, and zooming into each schema. At each stage, we are making a decision which collapses the various spaces of potential, into an abstracted, automated form. 

Information can be Entropy, Abstraction and Automation reduces Entropy.

It is somewhat preposterous, becuase at an external scale; most of my local work requires writing literature, researching, coding and organizing these systems. This seems laughably primitive as compared to a Statistician's job; and that is exactly the aim of this discussion. I suspect, regardless of the stark differences in superficial workflow; the core problem is something approximating an axiom-sytsem of; information-communication-presentation-organization-management-navigation. This unifies both the Statistician's Job, and my local writing work.

I can think of one example during the Wars of the past century; where one man single-handedly aborted a missile-strike, which most likely would have engaged "M.A.D"

That is fundamentally an Ontological issue of "Phenomenology-and-Information"; decision-making -> communication -> interpretation -> information transmission & transfer -> source, emission, origin -> event

For this vein of inquiry, Vannevar Bush's "As We May Think" is an integral material for this study, even moreso considering the era of his writing; post-war civilization. Where science was no longer an intellectual instrument of war; but an open field of potential. That potential, needs Ethics, and such Ethics, cannot be arbitrarily defined in a document or law; they must source from the Phenomenological, the Organic.
